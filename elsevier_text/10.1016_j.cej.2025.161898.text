[Paragraph 11]
Graphical abstract

      

A novel porous CoS2@NiS2@C@NC (CNSNC) concave cubic nanobox composite was designed as a cathode material for aluminum batteries, obtained by modifying CoS2@NiS2 (CNS) with skeleton carbon and nitrogen-carbon co-doping. The CNSNC-400 formed by calcination at 400 ℃ exhibits excellent discharge capacity and cycle stability. At a constant current density of 0.3 A/g, its first cycle capacity is about 301 mAh/g, and the capacity remains at about 218 mAh/g after 200 cycles. At the same time, even at 2 A/g, its capacity can also be stabilized at 99 mAh/g. The improvement in material performance is mainly attributed to the heterojunction formed between NiS2 and CoS2, which forms an internal electric field at the interface, which is beneficial to the transport of ions. On the other hand, the nitrogen doping, skeleton carbon and carbon coating improve its conductivity. The internal porous structure and external carbon coating maintain the stability of the structure. DFT calculation results further confirmed that the CoS2@NiS2 heterojunction exhibits a lower migration barrier for Al3+ ions, which is beneficial to the diffusion of Al3+ ions and improves the electrochemical reaction kinetics.

Introduction

Excessive development and use of non-renewable energy such as fossil energy are having far-reaching effects on our climate and ecosystems and causing human and environmental health problems []. Battery technology is becoming an important tool to promote energy transformation, help decarbonize transportation and energy, and combat climate change []. The exponential growth of electric vehicles (EV) and energy storage system (ESS) applications is the greatest contributor to increasing demand for lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). The rapid growth of demand for these batteries could put great pressure on supply chains for critical materials like lithium. Therefore, it is urgent to explore and develop advanced new battery technology. Aluminum batteries (ABs) have great potential for future energy storage systems due to their low flammability, faster charging speed, moderately higher energy density, easier recycling and inexpensive [,]. To improve the storable energy and efficiency of ABs, the performance is mainly limited by the properties of the cathode materials, among other factors. At present, the cathode materials of ABs can be roughly classified into the following categories: sulfides [,], oxides [,], selenides [,], elemental sulfur [], organic polymers [] and carbon materials [,]. Sulfides such as NiS2 and CoS2 have the advantages of high discharge capacity and large interlayer spacing, but they suffer from drawbacks like poor conductivity and structural collapse during the charge-discharge process [,].

[Paragraph 14]
Carbon materials are widely recognized for their superior electrical conductivity and robust structural stability. By incorporating carbon materials into sulfide composites, it is possible to mitigate the inherent limitations of sulfides, such as poor conductivity and structural instability. For instance, Yang et al. reported a composite material composed of bimetallic-sulfide (SnCo)S2 nanocubes and 2D sulfur-doped graphene (SG) nanosheets []. It exhibits an excellent rate performance and durability. Hu [] et al. investigated a binder-free and free-standing Co9S8@CNTs composite as a cathode material for AIBs. This material exhibited high specific capacity, excellent rate performance, and remarkable cycling stability. This strategy significantly enhanced the cycling lifespan of AIBs. Yang [] et al. synthesized single-/few-layered WS2 nanoplates using a specific synthesis method and uniformly dispersed them in nitrogen-doped carbon nanofibers (N-CNFs), forming a stable composite structure. This composite effectively prevents the aggregation of WS2, enhancing the electrode's conductivity and structural stability. It provides insights into addressing the agglomeration issue commonly observed in ABs cathode materials. In addition, Zhou et al. employed a carbon coating strategy to synthesize a willow-leaf-shaped ReSe2@C composite material [], which exhibits superior rate capability and cycling stability compared to most reported cathode materials for AIBs. This carbon coating approach enhances the electrical conductivity of the material, effectively addressing the inherently poor conductivity of ReSe2. Furthermore, it reinforces structural stability, mitigating volume expansion and material pulverization during cycling. Cai [] et al. synthesized a CoSe2/C-ND@rGO composite via a combination of hydrothermal and thermal treatment methods. This composite exhibits a high specific capacity, outstanding rate capability, and long-term cycling stability in AIBs, surpassing conventional electrode materials. The structural design effectively mitigates volumetric expansion, thereby enhancing the battery's cycle life. Through the reports of the above researchers, it can be seen that the properties of the electrode materials have been significantly improved after the modification of carbon materials.

[Paragraph 15]
In summary, we designed a novel porous CoS2@NiS2@C@NC (CNSNC) concave cubic nanobox composite as the cathode material for ABs, which was obtained by modifying CoS2@NiS2 (CNS) with skeleton carbon and nitrogen-carbon co-doping. The CNSNC-400 formed by calcination at 400 ℃ as the cathode of ABs possesses excellent capacity, diffusion kinetic and cycle stability. The excellent performance of CNSNC-400 is attributed to the synergistic effect of the following two aspects. On the one hand, the heterojunction formed between CoS2 and NiS2 will produce lattice mismatch at the interface, which is conducive to the transmission of ions. On the other hand, the nitrogen doping, the skeletal carbon and carbon coating layer of CNSNC-400 improve its conductivity. In addition, the carbon coating layer can prevent the structural collapse and material agglomeration of CNSNC-400, thereby improving its structural stability.

Experimentation

 Chemical reagents: Potassium cobalt cyanide (K3[Co(CN)6]), nickel nitrate (Ni(NO3)2), sodium citrate (C6H5Na3O7), Tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (C4H11NO3), dopamine hydrochloride (C8H11NO2⋅HCl), sublimed sulfur (S) powder, ethanol (C2H6O2), aluminum chloride (AlCl3), 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride ([EMIm]Cl), acetylene black, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), N-methyl pyrrolidone (NMP). The above reagents can be used directly without further purification.

[Paragraph 17]
Synthesis of CNCP NBs: Cobalt-nickel polymer precursor NBs (named CNCP NBs) were synthesized by co-precipitation method. Typically, 1.2 mmol of K3[Co(CN)6] was dissolved in 60 ml water, named A solution; 1.8 mmol of Ni(NO3)2 and 2.7 mmol of sodium citrate were dissolved in 60 ml water, named B solution. Then, B was poured into A and stirred for 10 min and left to stand for 24 h. CNCP NBs was obtained by centrifugation, washing and drying.

[Paragraph 18]
Synthesis of CNCP@PDA NBs: Firstly, 0.1211 g of C4H11NO3 was added to 100 ml of water, and then 0.1 g of CNCP NBs was dispersed in the above solution. After ultrasonication for 20 min, 0.025 g of dopamine hydrochloride was added and stirred for 24 h. Subsequently, CNCP@PDA NBs were obtained by centrifugation and washing with water for several times.

[Paragraph 19]
Synthesis of CNSNC NBs: The pre-prepared CNCP@PDA and S powder were mixed and ground at a mass ratio of 1:3 for 10 min, and then calcined in a tube furnace. Afterwards, CoS2@NiS2@C@NC-400 NBs (named CNSNC-400 NBs) was obtained after calcination at 400 °C for 3 h at a heating rate of 2 °C/min in argon atmosphere. For comparison, CNCP@PDA and S were calcined at 500 and 600 °C to obtain CNSNC-500 and CNSNC-600, respectively.

[Paragraph 20]
Synthesis of CNSC NBs: The preparation method of CoS2@NiS2@C NBs (named CNSC NBs) is the same as that of CNSNC NBs, only CNCP@PDA is replaced by CNCP.

 Synthesis of electrolyte: In the glove box, AlCl3 and [EMIm]Cl (molar ratio of 1.3: 1) were stirred for 12 h to obtain AIB electrolyte.

Assembly of pouch battery

The assembly process for pouch batteries can be found in our previous paper [], only the preparation of the positive electrode is different. During the preparation of the electrode, the cathode material CNSNC (CNSC), acetylene black and PVDF were mixed and stirred at a mass ratio of 7:2:1, and then an appropriate amount of NMP organic solvent was added and stirred for 12 h. Subsequently, the obtained slurry was coated on the molybdenum (Mo) sheet and dried for 12 h to obtain the CNSNC (CNSC) electrode. After drying, the cathode sheets are cut into dimensions of 2 × 2 cm for later use. Aluminum sheets are trimmed to 3 × 3 cm and subsequently immersed in 1 mol/L hydrochloric acid for approximately 10 min before being rinsed with deionized water. Additionally, Whatman separators are cut to 3.5 × 3.5 cm, while aluminum-plastic films are prepared in 6 × 10 cm dimensions. Finally, under an argon atmosphere, these components are assembled into a pouch battery using a heat-sealing machine.

[Paragraph 21]
Characterization test

The crystal structure and phase composition of the material were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Raman spectroscopy. Thermogravimetric (TG) test was carried out from room temperature to 800 ℃ in air at a heating rate of 10 ℃/min. The element valence of the synthesized materials was analyzed by XPS. The specific surface area of the synthesized materials was tested by BET physical adsorption meter. The morphology and microstructure of the materials were observed by SEM and TEM. Cyclic voltammogram (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were tested by a Chi660e workstation. The cycle performance and rate performance were tested by the Neware system. Instrument models and conditions for all characterization tests are visible in our previous reports [].

[Paragraph 22]
DFT calculation

The generalized gradient approximation by Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) was employed to describe the exchange-correlation functional []. The ion-electron interaction was estimated by the projector augmented wave model []. The energy cutoff for the plane wave expansion was set to 450 eV. The energy convergence for terminating the electronic self-consistent field was 10-6 eV/atom, and the force convergence on each atom for geometric optimization was 10-2 eV/Å.

Results and discussion

The preparation process and crystal structure model of porous CNSNC-400 NB is briefly explained by the schematic diagram. As shown in  , CNCP NBs were first prepared by a coordination reaction, followed by coating with a PDA layer on the surface of CNCP under the action of chemical additives, and finally, porous CNSNC-400 NBs were obtained by calcinating CNCP@PDA. The presence of lattice mismatch at the CoS2@NiS2 heterojunction interface induces an internal electric field, which is conducive to the transmission of ions.

[Paragraph 24]
XRD test was performed using Cu Ka radiation at an angle of 10-80°. The XRD diffraction peaks of CNCP in  a can be attributed to the standard PDF card (#01-076-5031). As shown in b, the XRD patterns of CNSC and CNSNC-400 match the standard card CoS2 (#01-071-4835) and NiS2 (#01-080-0375), both of which belong to the cubic crystal system. XRD patterns of CNSC and CNSNC-400 show nearly identical peak positions, suggesting that the carbon coating minimally affects the crystal structure of CNSC. From a and 2b, there are no other impurity peaks in the XRD patterns, indicating that the pre-synthesized materials are single-phase CNCP, CNSC, and CNSNC-400. In addition, the crystal structure of the products obtained by calcining CNCP@PDA at different temperatures was also explored. The standard PDF card corresponding to the products CNSNC-500 and CNSNC-600 obtained at 500 and 600 ℃ is CoNi2S4 (#01-073-1297), which can be seen in c. Interestingly, when the calcination temperature is 500 and 600 °C, the crystal type of CNSNC has changed, but their crystals belong to the cubic crystal system. At approximately 15°, 30°, 35° and 46°, impurity diffraction peaks were observed in c. Comparison with XRD reference patterns identified the peak at 15° as belonging to C (PDF#00-050-0927), while the remaining impurity peaks were attributed to NiS (PDF#00-002-1280). This phenomenon can be explained by the loss of an S atom from NiS2 at 500 °C, leading to the formation of NiS. As the temperature further increased to 600 °C, significant carbonization occurred, resulting in the prominent C peak.

[Paragraph 25]
The phase compositions of CNSC and CNSNC-400 were further analyzed by Raman to characterize the carbon materials. As shown in the Raman spectra of CNSC and CNSNC-400 in d, the Raman bands belonging to CoS2 and NiS2 were observed in the range of 100-800 cm-1. The Raman characteristic peak of the Eg vibration mode belonging to CoS2 is visible at the band of 377 cm-1 []. In addition, the peaks of CNSC and CNSNC-400 at 661 and 505 cm-1 can be attributed to the A1g and F2g vibration modes of Co3O4, respectively, which may be due to partial surface oxidation caused by laser heating during the test []. The two Raman characteristic peaks observed at 473 and 269 cm-1 correspond to the Ag and Eg photons of NiS2, respectively [,]. Additionally, the extra peak observed at 187 cm-1 for both CNSC and CNSNC-400 can be attributed to the oxidation of a portion of Ni element []. The characteristic Raman peaks of pre-synthesized CNSC and CNSNC-400 were observed at about 1370 and 1530 cm-1, which belong to the D and G bands of carbon material, respectively. This suggests that carbon materials exist in both CNSC and CNSNC-400 samples. By calculation, the ratio of D and G band intensity (ID/IG) of CNSNC-400 is 1.09, and the ID/IG of CNSC is 1. Using the four-probe method, the electrical conductivities of CoS2@NiS2, CNSC, and CNSNC-400 were measured as 1.1 × 10-3, 1.7 × 10-2, and 4.8 × 10-1 S/cm, respectively. Therefore, the electrical conductivity of CoS2@NiS2 is well improved due to the simultaneous presence of two modified carbon materials in CNSNC-400. TG curves of CNSC and CNSNC-400 were obtained by heating test in air, as shown in e. When the heating temperature is raised to 800 ℃, there is no further loss of mass. It can be seen that at this point the mass of CNSC and CNSNC-400 is 58.9 % and 43.6 %, respectively, so that the content of coated carbon in CNSNC-400 is about 15.3 %. The N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms of CNSC and CNSNC-400 were measured within a relative pressure range of 0 to 1 (f). The BET surface area of CNSC and CNSNC-400 were determined to be 63.1 and 106.5 m2/g, respectively. Compared to CNSC, the BET surface area of CNSNC-400 is much improved. Therefore, the coated carbon provides more contact area, which can improve the infiltration rate of the electrolyte to the CNSNC-400 electrode material.

[Paragraph 26]
For exploring the element valence and surface chemical composition of CNSNC-400 composite, XPS measurements were performed. The XPS survey spectrum ( a) contains five elements of C, N, Ni, Co and S, which is consistent with the XRD result. With the Gaussian fitting method, Ni 2p can be fitted to two spin-orbit double peaks and two oscillating satellite peaks (named as "Sat."). For Ni 2p (b), the peaks of Ni 2p3/2 at 856.3 eV and Ni 2p1/2 at 874.6 eV can be assigned to Ni2+, and the peaks at 858.3 eV (Ni 2p3/2) and 878.4 eV (Ni 2p1/2) are related to Ni3+ []. Similarly, the peak at 781.9 eV in c belongs to Co 2p3/2, while the characteristic peak at 798.1 eV is related to Co 2p1/2 []. The peaks located at 786.1 and 803.1 eV correspond to the satellite peaks of Co 2p. As for S 2p (d), the strong peaks near 162.1 eV and 163.8 eV are attributed to S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2, respectively, corresponding to metal-sulfur (M-S, M=Co, Ni) bonds []. The characteristic peak at the binding energy of 165.5 eV is related to the C-S-C bond, indicating that S atoms are bonded to the skeleton carbon in CNSNC-400 []. The satellite peak at 168.3 eV is related to the sulfur atom in the low coordination state on the surface []. In addition, nitrogen-carbon co-doping was characterized by C 1 s (e) and N 1 s (f) spectra. The peak at 286.2 eV in the C 1 s spectrum can be attributed to the C-N bond [], which in combination with the three peaks corresponding to pyridinic N (398.5 eV), pyrrolic N (400.1 eV), and graphitic N (401.4 eV) [] in the N 1 s spectrum suggests that there is simultaneous carbon-nitrogen co-doping of CNSNC-400 []. As shown in e, the peaks at 284.9 eV and 288.5 eV in the C 1 s spectrum belong to C-C and C-S bonds, respectively []. Combined with the C 1 s and S 2p spectra, the covalent bond between C and S atoms can be strongly proved.

[Paragraph 27]
The XPS spectral comparison of CoS2, NiS2, and CNSNC-400 is presented in b, 3c. The Co 2p3/2 peak shifts from 783.4 eV to 781.9 eV, while the Co 2p1/2 peak moves from 799.1 eV to 798.1 eV, signifying an increase in the electronic density of Co2+. Conversely, the binding energies of Ni2+ 2p3/2, Ni3+ 2p3/2, Ni2+ 2p1/2, and Ni3+ 2p1/2 exhibit positive shifts from 855 eV to 856.3 eV, 857.5 eV to 858.3 eV, 873 eV to 874.6 eV, and 877.3 eV to 878.4 eV, respectively. These shifts provide direct evidence of electron transfer from NiS2 to CoS2. These energy variations result from heterophase interfaces accompanied by lattice distortion. The spontaneous electron migration from NiS2 to CoS2, facilitated by lattice strain and defect sites, induces interfacial charge redistribution. The increased electron density in CoS2 enhances Al3+ adsorption, thereby promoting faster reaction kinetics [].

[Paragraph 28]
For the purpose of further exploring the intrinsic properties of the synthetic materials, the morphology and structure of CNCP, CNCP@PDA, CNSC and CNSNC obtained at different annealing temperatures were observed and analyzed using SEM and TEM. The morphology of CNCP exhibits a uniform cubic structure with a particle size of about 400 nm, which can be observed from  a-4d. The PDA coating layer can be clearly observed through e-4 h, thus confirming the formation of the CNCP@PDA NBs composite material. It can be seen from the TEM image of h that the thickness of the PDA coating layer is about 20 nm. Subsequently, the sample CNSC obtained by calcining CNCP was analyzed by SEM and TEM. As shown in i-4 k, CNSC still maintains the uniform cubic shape of CNCP, but its structure changes from cube to concave cube. It can be observed from k-4 g that the center of CNSC shows obvious inward depression, forming a three-dimensional six-concave nanobox structure with a large number of micropores, and carbon is distributed throughout the internal structure. The two illustrations in o show that in the HRTEM image of CNSC, the lattice fringes are 0.281 nm and 0.326 nm, corresponding to the crystal planes of NiS2 (200) and CoS2 (111), respectively. At the same time, amorphous carbon is distributed around NiS2 and CoS2, forming a conductive network. Therefore, combined with i-4o, it can be seen that the CNSC concave cubic box is composed of many CoS2@NiS2 and C nanoparticles, which are interlaced with each other. This means that there is a carbon distribution in the structure of the whole CNSC, so it is called skeleton carbon. As shown in the selected area diffraction diagram of p, the diffraction ring corresponding to the crystal planes belongs to the (400) and (311) crystal planes of NiS2 and CoS2, respectively, which indicates that CNSC is a polycrystalline structure. A TEM mapping test was performed on n, as shown in q. It can be seen that Ni, Co, S, C and N elements are evenly distributed in CNSC, which indicates the successful preparation of CoS2@NiS2@C. Interestingly, the internal distribution of C and N elements is relatively sparse, indicating the formation of skeletal carbon.

[Paragraph 29]
The samples obtained by calcining CNCP@PDA at different temperatures were also characterized by SEM and TEM. The morphology of sample CNSNC-600 calcined at 600 ℃ changed to irregular lumpy particles ( a), which was due to the high calcination temperature. The CNSNC-500 maintains the cubic shape of CNCP@PDA (b), but the structure is slightly deformed and the thickness of carbon coating becomes uneven. The TEM image of c shows that the internal structure of CNSNC-500 NBs is slightly deformed, but the structure of the outermost carbon layer remains intact, indicating that the carbonized PDA coating has a good protective effect on the internal structure. d is the HRTEM image of CNSNC-500, and there is an obvious interplanar spacing at 0.333 nm, which belongs to the typical (220) crystal plane of CoNi2S4. Combined with XRD (c) and HRTEM (d) analysis, CNSNC-500 and CNSNC-600 seem to be understood as CoS2@NiS2 losing part of S atom at high temperature to form CoNi2S4 substance. e-g show the SEM images of CNSNC-400 NBs at different magnifications, and like CNSC, CNSNC-400 also exhibits a concave cubic box structure. Despite the concavity that occurs on each face of CNSNC-400, it still preserves a similar size and cubic box shape to CNCP@PDA. It can be judged from h-j that CNSNC-400 NBs have an internal porous structure, and these pores can increase the specific surface area of CNSNC-400 NBs, which is conducive to the transfer of Al3+ ion. It also can be observed from j that CNSNC-400 has a transparent carbon layer on the outside (thickness of about 5-20 nm) and many carbon nanoparticles (the elliptical marker) inside, which suggests the simultaneous presence of two carbon modifications in CNSNC-400. The microstructure of CNSNC-400 was further characterized by HRTEM, as shown in k. The interplanar spacing located at 0.324 nm and 0.326 nm can be attributed to the (111) plane of NiS2 and the (111) plane of CoS2, respectively, and the boundary formed between the two crystal planes suggests that a heterojunction was successfully constructed between NiS2 and CoS2. It can be seen that the amorphous carbon is interspersed with the crystalline planes of NiS2 and CoS2, while heterojunctions are formed between NiS2 and CoS2, suggesting that the three constitute a complex crosslinked structure within CNSNC-400 NBs. The SEM and TEM images (e-k) demonstrate that CNSNC-400 is composed of C and CoS2@NiS2 nanoparticles that are staggered inside the nitrogen-doped carbon coating layer. Similar to CNSC, these C nanoparticles are combined to form a carbon skeleton inside CNSNC-400, indicating that CNSNC-400 has both skeleton carbon and coated carbon modified carbon. From the SAED image of l, it can be seen that CNSNC-400 is a polycrystalline structure, and the two diffraction rings correspond to the (211) and (321) crystal planes of NiS2 and CoS2, respectively. The element mapping test of TEM was performed on i, as shown in m. Ni, Co, S, C and N elements are uniformly distributed in CNSNC-400 NBs. Interestingly, the distribution area of C and N elements is wider than that of the other three elements, which further confirms the existence of external coated carbon. Nitrogen-carbon co-doping is achieved by the decomposition of cyanide ligands in CNCP and the pyrolysis of dopamine.

For the purpose of investigating the electrochemical reaction mechanism of CNSNC-400 as the cathode material of AB, the CV curve was tested for three cycles at 0.6 mV/s. Two pairs of redox peaks of 1.69 V/1.05 V and 1.07 V/0.51 V can be observed from  a. The reduction peak (located at 1.05 V) in the cathodic scan corresponds to the reaction of Al3+ embedded in the structure of CoS2 to form AlzCoS2 []. The reduction peak located at 0.51 V corresponds to the reaction in which Al3+ is embedded into the structure of NiS2 to form AlxNiS2 []. When the cathodic scan proceeds to the 0.01 V potential, the discharge process ends and the charging process begins to be executed. The oxidation peak at 1.07 V in the anodic scan is related to the reaction that occurs when Al3+ is de-embedded from AlxNiS2. Similarly, the oxidation peak located at 1.69 V is related to the reaction that occurs when Al3+ is de-embedded from AlzCoS2. It can be learned that the reactions occurring at the reduction peaks at 1.05 and 0.51 V and the oxidation peaks at 1.69 and 1.07 V are reverse reactions. When the anodic scan is carried out to 2.3 V, the structure of CNSNC-400 returns to the initial state. In the three-cycle CV curve, the peak value of redox peaks did not shift too much, indicating that CNSNC-400 has a good structural stability.

[Paragraph 32]
Based on the unique porous concave cubic structure of CNSC and CNSNC-400, their electrochemical performance as cathode materials for ABs were further studied. The plateau of the charge/discharge curve of CNSNC-400 in b coincides with the redox peak of the CV curve in a, indicating that CNSNC-400 has a good reversibility. b and 6c are the charge-discharge curves of CNSC and CNSNC-400 at a constant current density of 0.3 A/g and the CV curves of CNSC and CNSNC-400 at a scan rate of 0.6 mV/s, respectively. It can be seen from the CV curves that the integral area of CNSNC-400 is significantly larger than that of CNSC, indicating that the former has a capacity advantage over the latter. In b and c, the reduction peak potential (1.05 V and 0.51 V) of CNSNC-400 is more positive than that of CNSC (0.92 V and 0.42 V), while the oxidation peak potential (1.69 V and 1.07 V) of CNSNC-400 is more negative than that of CNSC (1.77 V and 1.18 V). The redox peak potential difference of CNSNC-400 is smaller than that of CNSC, indicating that CNSNC-400 electrode exhibits a better reversibility of redox reaction. In order to further study the improvement of carbon layer on the performance of CNSNC-400, the cycle performance and rate performance of CNSC, CNSNC-400, CNSNC-500 and CNSNC-600 were tested. The rate capacities (e) of CNSC, CNSNC-400, CNSNC-500 and CNSNC-600 at five current densities (0.3 A/g, 0.5 A/g, 1 A/g, 1.5 A/g and 2 A/g) were tested in the range of 0.1-2.3 V. It can be seen that the capacity of CNSNC-400 is the highest and the capacity decay of CNSNC-400 is less than the other three electrode materials when the current density goes back to 0.3 A/g, indicating that CNSNC-400 possesses a superior rate performance. At the same time, CNSNC-400 can provide a discharge capacity of nearly 99 mAh/g at a high constant current density of 2 A/g. f is the cycle performance diagram of CNSC, CNSNC-400, CNSNC-500 and CNSNC-600 electrode materials at a current density of 0.3 A/g. The initial capacities of CNSC, CNSNC-400, CNSNC-500 and CNSNC-600 are 266, 301, 261 and 159 mAh/g, respectively. After 200 cycles, their capacities are 106, 218, 99 and 27 mAh/g, respectively. Among them, the capacity retention rate of CNSNC-400 (72.4 %) is significantly higher than that of CNSC (39.7 %), CNSNC-500 (37.9 %) and CNSNC-600 (17 %). In addition, the overall coulombic efficiency (CE) of CNSNC-400 (close to 100 %) is higher than that of CNSC, CNSNC-500 and CNSNC-600, and the fluctuation range is also the smallest. It can be seen from the above results that the satisfactory structural stability of CNSNC-400 is closely related to the carbon coating layer and nitrogen doping. As shown in d, the performance of CNSNC-400 and other cathode materials for ABs was compared (e.g., Co3S4 [], Cu7S4@PDA [], MoO2@Ni [], Ni3S2@GO [] and SnSe2@NiSe2 []). Among them, the current density of CNSNC-400 is the largest, and its first cycle capacity and capacity retention rate are also the highest, further reflecting its dramatic performance. The reasons for the excellent electrochemical performance of CNSNC-400 can be summarized as follows: (i) CNSNC-400 has an internal porous structure and an external carbon coating layer, which greatly increases its specific surface area, thereby increasing the contact area between the electrode material and the electrolyte and providing a lot of electrochemical reaction sites. (ii) The skeleton carbon network, carbon coating layer and nitrogen doping of CNSNC-400 improve its conductivity and accelerate the transport of ions/electrons during charging and discharging. (iii) The CNSNC-400 cube is composed of numerous nanoparticles, which are beneficial to the diffusion of Al3+ ions. (iv) The carbon coating layer can reduce the degree of corrosion of the internal structure by the electrolyte, prevent structural collapse and material agglomeration, thereby improving the structural stability of the electrode material. (v) The heterojunction formed between NiS2 and CoS2 will produce lattice mismatch at the interface, which is beneficial to the transmission of ions. (vi) The incorporation of a carbon layer and nitrogen doping effectively mitigates side reactions and reduces polarization losses, thereby enhancing the cycling stability and CE of the electrode material.

[Paragraph 33]
For the purpose of further investigating the effect of carbon coating on the performance of CNSNC-400, the pseudocapacitive properties of CNSC and CNSNC-400 were compared and analyzed. As shown in  a and e, CV measurements were performed using five scan rates (0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1 mV/s) from 0.01 V to 2.3 V. The relationship between peak current (i) and scanning speed (v) can be expressed by the following power exponential function []:  (4)   i  =    av  b

[Paragraph 34]
The b value in Eq.  is a parameter used to distinguish between surface-induced and diffusion control capacitive behavior []. When the b value is closer to 1, the proportion of capacitance behavior in the charge storage mechanism is higher. On the contrary, if the b value is close to 0.5, the diffusion-controlled capacitive behavior dominates. As shown in b and 8f, the slope of the straight line obtained by fitting the peak current of the redox peak with the scan rate is the value of b. The b-values of the redox peaks of CNSNC-400 are 0.83, 0.73, 0.71 and 0.66, respectively, while those of CNSC are 0.64, 0.61, 0.58 and 0.55, respectively, indicating that their electrochemical reactions are mainly dominated by pseudocapacitive behavior. Additionally, CNSNC-400 exhibits a larger b value compared to CNSC, indicating a higher capacitive contribution to its charge storage mechanism. Knowing the charge storage mechanisms of CNSNC-400 and CNSC from the above analysis, the following equations are further used to quantitatively determine the contribution of capacitance []:  (5)   i  =   k 1  v  +   k 2    v   0.5

[Paragraph 35]

It can be seen from c and d that the proportion of pseudocapacitance at the five scan rates of CNSNC-400 is 41.8 %, 63.9 %, 70.2 %, 79.3 % and 86.4 %, respectively. This means that when the scanning speed increases to a certain extent, the proportion of pseudocapacitance will be close to 100 %, and the charge storage mechanism will be completely dominated by capacitance behavior. For comparison, the proportion of pseudocapacitances (g and h) of CNSC at five scan rates are 34.6 %, 47.9 %, 60.1 %, 67.2 % and 75.3 %, respectively. As a result, the CNSNC-400 composite modified with carbon coating have a larger pseudocapacitance ratio than CNSC at the same scan rate. At the same time, the integral area of CV curve of CNSNC-400 is larger than that of CNSC at each scanning speed, which indicates that CNSNC-400 composite material has capacity advantage. Since the capacitive contribution can provide a fast ion storage mechanism, thus the CNSNC-400 cathode can charge/discharge faster in ABs than CNSC. [Paragraph 39]
The formation of the CoS2@NiS2 heterojunction can be observed through the HRTEM image in k. Therefore, the interface structure of the CoS2@NiS2 heterojunction and the transport property of Al3+ ions at the interface were studied by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Both CoS2 and NiS2 crystals exhibit a cubic crystal structure, with their strongest diffraction peak corresponding to the (200) plane as determined by XRD analysis. Consequently, a composite structure was created by stacking layers of CoS2 and NiS2, aligning their (200) crystal plane. In fact, various model constructions were attempted to build heterostructure interfaces with other possible crystal planes of CoS2 and NiS2, such as their (220) and (311) planes. However, the constructed heterojunctions could not form stable structures. Through several geometric optimizations, a CoS2@NiS2 heterostructure was constructed by aligning the (200) plane of CoS2 with the (200) plane of NiS2, resulting in a 45° dislocation at the interface. The optimized primitive cell of this heterostructure can be represented by Co6Ni6S24. As shown in  a, the differential charge of the heterostructure was simulated, and the in-plane average charge density showed a strong charge transfer at the heterostructure interface. Simultaneously, a significant number of interatomic gaps can be observed at the CoS2@NiS2 interface. The diameter of these gaps is approximately 2.34 Å (b), significantly larger than the ionic diameter of Al3+ (1.02 Å), therefore these gaps are conducive to the storage and diffusion of Al3+ ions. The 1 × 1 supercell of CoS2@NiS2 heterojunction was further used to verify the capacity property of Al3+ ions. In c, a possible diffusion pathway for Al3+ ions can be found, namely along the [0 1 0] direction. The obtained energy distribution of Al3+ ions is shown in d, and the migration energy barrier (Ebar) of this pathway is about 0.496 eV. Compared to traditional MnO2 cathode (0.512 eV) [], the CoS2@NiS2 heterojunction exhibits a lower migration energy barrier for Al3+ ions. Simultaneously, this energy value is comparable to the diffusion energy barriers of typical cathode materials for lithium-ion batteries, such as the metal halide perovskite MASnCl3 (0.45 eV) [], Li4GeS4 (0.53 eV) [], and γ-Li3PS4 (0.49 eV) []. In conclusion, this CoS2@NiS2 heterojunction facilitates Al3+ ion diffusion and enhances electrochemical reaction kinetics.

[Paragraph 40]
Conclusion

In summary, we constructed and prepared the heterojunction structure of CoS2 and NiS2, and obtained porous CNSNC-400 NB composites by modifying CoS2@NiS2 with skeleton carbon and nitrogen-carbon co-doping. Attributed to the double carbon modification, CNSNC-400 as the cathode of ABs possesses an excellent electrochemical performance. Compared with CNSC, CNSNC-500 and CNSNC-600, CNSNC-400 shows the best cycle stability, discharge capacity and diffusion kinetic. The skeletal carbon and carbon coating layer of CNSNC-400 improve its conductivity, prevent its structural collapse and material agglomeration, and improve its structural stability. The lattice mismatch at the interface of the CoS2@NiS2 heterojunction generates an internal electric field, which promotes ion transport. DFT calculation results show that CoS2@NiS2 heterojunction exhibits excellent diffusion performance for Al3+ ion and improves the electrochemical reaction kinetics.
