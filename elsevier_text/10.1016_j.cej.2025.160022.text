Graphical abstract

A unique bimetal-porous carbon network developed as a flexible self-supporting sulfur host for high-performance lithium-sulfur batteries, enhancement of rate performance by synergizing bimetallic bidirectional catalysis and high conductivity of CNT.

[Paragraph 10]
The commercial application of flexible lithium-sulfur batteries is severely hindered by poor conductivity, low active material loading, polysulfide shuttle effects, and sluggish redox kinetics. Herein, we developed a unique three-dimensional (3D) conductive network framework decorated with NiCo bimetallic particles. The 3D porous carbon conductive network skeleton formed by crosslinked carbon nanotubes effectively mitigates the volume expansion of sulfur and accommodates abundant active materials for high discharge capacity. Meanwhile, the NiCo bimetallic combines the catalytic role of Ni in sulfur reduction and Co in sulfur oxidation to achieve improved kinetics of the entire conversion of sulfur. Most importantly, the sulfur host synergizes the conducting CNTs and catalytically active bimetallic, increasing conductance and accelerating reaction kinetics, resulting in a significant improvement in rate performance. Consequently, the cells equipped with NiCo@CNT-S cathode exhibit a low-capacity decay rate of only 0.07 % per cycle over 400 cycles at 1C, and an ultra-high initial specific capacity of 1030 mAh g-1 at a high rate of 5C. Notably, the pouch cell assembled with the NiCo@CNT-S could deliver a high areal discharge capacity of 8.2 mAh cm-2 at 0.1C, with a sulfur loading of 7.1 mg cm-2 and an E/S ratio of 7.1 μL mg-1. This work provides novel structural design and mechanism insights for the practical application of flexible lithium-sulfur batteries.

Introduction

In recent years, the widespread demand for flexible wearable electronics and portable medical devices has driven the rapid development of high-capacity, high-rate, non-toxic, and flexible energy storage devices. Among various battery systems, lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries are one of the most promising rechargeable batteries for flexible energy storage devices due to their high energy density (2600 Wh kg-1), low cost, and environmental friendliness. Despite these advantages, Li-S batteries face several challenges in practical applications: i) The low conductivity of sulfur and its discharge products (Li2S/Li2S2) restricts lithium-ion diffusion in the cathode, slowing redox reaction kinetics and impairing rate performance; ii) The large volume change of sulfur during charge and discharge (approximately 80 %) damages the electrode structure integrity; iii) The shuttle effect of lithium polysulfides (LiPSs) inevitably leads to continuous active material loss, causing rapid capacity decay during cycling.

[Paragraph 13]
In the past decades, numerous approaches have been explored to tackle these issues, such as constructing multifunctional cathode structures, designing membrane coatings, and optimizing electrolyte additives. Among them, the rational design of porous carbon-based materials is one of the most effective methods to address the poor conductivity of sulfur and its volume expansion during charging and discharging. Examples include carbon nanotubes (CNTs), hollow spheres, graphene, etc, due to their high porosity, large specific surface area, and customizable structure, which not only effectively alleviate the volume expansion of sulfur but also accommodate more active materials, thus promoting the practical applicability of Li-S batteries. Unfortunately, these bare carbons are limited by their nonpolar surface chemistry and weak interactions with polar LiPSs, resulting in insufficient adsorption capacity and exacerbating the shuttle effect of LiPSs. Moreover, the sulfur reduction reaction (SRR) and sulfur oxidation reaction (SOR) each involve a complex solid-liquid-solid process with a 16electron transfer. In this process, nucleation and dissolution of Li2S are critical steps in SRR and SOR determining the overall reaction mechanism. Due to the relatively slow kinetics of the SRR and SOR, the LiPSs tend to accumulate in the electrolyte, exacerbating the shuttling effect of LiPSs. It has been shown that some transition metals-based composites (e.g., Ni, Co, Fe, etc.) are capable of accelerating the Li2S nucleation-dissolution rate via an adsorption-catalytic strategy, effectively inhibiting the shuttle effect of sulfur species and enhancing the conversion kinetics. It is mainly due to the high electrical conductivity, enhanced chemisorption capacity, excellent catalytic activity, and favorable chemical stability of these transition metals-based composites. However, some challenges are still presented through the use of these transition metal-based composites: i) The relatively singular catalytic properties of these transition metals are not sufficient for complex SRRs and SORs involving 16 electron transfer. ii) Although these transition metal matrix composites offer relatively good electrical conductivity, they fail to satisfy the performance requirements of Li-S batteries at high rates.

[Paragraph 14]
Herein, we have developed a unique bimetal-porous carbon network skeleton composite structure based on carbon cloth (CC) as a flexible self-supporting sulfur host, which consists of a 3D CNTs conductive skeleton with NiCo bimetallic particles encapsulated inside. Specifically, the porous carbon conductive network skeleton formed by crosslinked CNTs effectively alleviates the sulfur volume expansion and accommodates abundant active materials to achieve high discharge capacity. Furthermore, the NiCo bimetallic particles can sufficiently anchor the LiPSs and accelerate the deposition-dissolution efficiency of Li2S, thus effectively inhibiting the shuttle effect and enhancing conversion kinetics. Most importantly, the sulfur host synergistically enhances the conducting properties of CNTs and the catalytic activity of bimetallic materials, leading to improved conductivity, accelerated reaction kinetics, and significantly enhanced rate performance. As a result,the cell equipped with NiCo@CNT-S cathode exhibits a low-capacity decay rate of only 0.07 % per cycle over 400 cycles at 1C, and an ultra-high initial specific capacity of 1030 mAh g-1 at 5C. Notably, the NiCo@CNT-S based pouch cell delivers a high areal discharge capacity of 8.2 mAh cm-2 at 0.1C, with a high sulfur loading of 7.1 mg cm-2 and a low E/S ratio of 7.1 μL mg-1.

Results and Discussion

   illustrates the preparation process of NiCo@CNT. Initially, the NiCo precursor is synthesized by a solvothermal method. Subsequently, Co2+ and Ni2+ are converted to NiCo2O4 during oxidation at 350 °C, while organic ligands react to produce CO2 and H2O, forming numerous micropores that increase growth sites for CNTs. Finally, the NiCo@CNT structure is synthesized via chemical vapor deposition (CVD). The NiCo2O4 is reduced to NiCo bimetals by introducing H2, while acetylene serves as the carbon source to grow NiCo bimetal-modified 3D interconnected conductive network skeleton.

[Paragraph 16]
Specifically, the SEM images of CC ( a and b) and NiCo precursor (c and d) reveal fish scale-like NiCo precursor structures forming on the initially smooth carbon fiber surface after solvothermal growth. After oxidation, the NiCo2O4 surface becomes roughened, with numerous holes forming across it (e and f). With the help of the NiCo bimetallic catalysts, a conductive network framework composed of numerous coarse CNTs and vine-like fine CNTs (g and h) develops across the CC surface after CVD process. Similarly, Ni@CNT (Fig. S1) and Co@CNT (Fig. S2) are grown on smooth CC surfaces for comparison. i-l present the energy dispersive X-ray (EDS) elemental mapping of NiCo@CNT, indicating the uniform distribution of C, Ni, and Co elements in the whole structure.

[Paragraph 17]
The microstructure of NiCo@CNT is further investigated bytransmission electron microscopy (TEM) at different magnifications. m shows that CNTs with diameters of approximately 10-100 nm intertwine with one another, encapsulating nanoparticles of corresponding sizes within the nanotube structures. TheHRTEMimage (n) shows that the 0.205 nm lattice stripe corresponds to the (111) crystal plane of the NiCo bimetals(JCPDS No. 04-0805 and JCPDS No. 15-0806) and the lattice fringes of 0.34 nm correspond to the (002) crystal plane of graphite carbon. In particular, the selected areaelectron diffraction(SAED) pattern (o) provides additional confirmation of the NiCo bimetals. Moreover, HAADF-STEM and the corresponding elemental mapping images (p-s) show a uniform distribution of C, Ni, and Co elements within the NiCo@CNT.

[Paragraph 18]
X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed to identify the crystalline structure and compositions of the CC, Ni@CNT, Co@CNT, and NiCo@CNT. As shown in  a, and Fig. S3, two broad peaks at 26° and 43° for all the samples are derived from graphitized carbon. The XRD patterns for NiCo@CNT exhibit two crystallized peaks located at 44.3°, 51.6°, and 76.1°, corresponding to the (111), (200), and (220) planes of Ni (PDF#04-0850) and Co (PDF#15-0806), similar to that of Ni@CNT and Co@CNT (Fig. S4). This is because Ni and Co atoms share the same face-centered cubic packing and a close atomic radius while facilitatingthe easy formation of NiCo bimetals. Therefore, XRD demonstrates the presence of NiCo bimetals in the carbon-based material without detectable impurities.

[Paragraph 19]
The chemical state and composition of the sample were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in b, the full XPS spectrum of NiCo@CNT confirms the presence of C, O, Co, and Ni. The high-resolution spectrum of C 1 s (c) shows a strong and sharp C-C peak at 284.8 eV, referring to the carbon matrix of CNT. Meanwhile, the C-O, C=O, and O-C-O species can be also detected, which could be attributed to partial surface oxidization and contamination. As shown in d, the high-resolution spectrum of Ni 2p consists of four fitted peaks, among which the peak centered at 852.6 eV belongs to Ni0 and two peaks located at 855.1 and 858.4 eV are attributed to Ni2+ and Ni3+. Moreover, a satellite peak is detected near 862.1 eV. Similarly, there is a peak at 778.6 eVin the high-resolution spectrum of Co 2p (e), which is assigned to Co0, and two peaks at 780.3 and 782.2 eV are ascribed to Co3+ and Co2+, respectively. Of these, the high oxidation states (Co2+, Co3+, Ni2+, and Ni3+) are attributable to surface oxidation of the metal in contact with air. Meanwhile, the high-resolution spectrum of O 1 s (f) further corroborates the existence of metal-O, C-O and C=O groups.

[Paragraph 20]
The electrochemical performance of the fabricated electrodes with Li2S6 catholyte as the active material was evaluated by assembling CR2032 coin cells in aLi-S battery setup.  a shows the long-cycle performance of Ni@CNT-S, Co@CNT-S, and NiCo@CNT-S based cells at 1C. The cell equipped with NiCo@CNT-S cathode exhibits an initial specific capacity as high as 1082 mAh g-1 at 1C, and a capacity decay rate of only 0.07 % per cycle over 400 cycles with an average coulombic efficiency of 98.4 %. b depicts the galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) profiles of the NiCo@CNT-S based cell at different cycles. Notably, the distinct two-plateau profile is maintained even after 400 cycles, indicating the highly reversible sulfur electrochemistry. Furthermore, the microstructural morphology of NiCo@CNT-S based cell remains intact after 100 cycles at 1C, demonstrating excellent structural stability (Fig. S5).

[Paragraph 21]
c shows the rate performance of Ni@CNT-S, Co@CNT-S, and NiCo@CNT-S based cells. Obviously, the cell equipped with NiCo@CNT-S cathode achieves the best rate performance, delivering discharge-specific capacities of 1442, 1338, 1225, 1147, 1072, 1011, and 886 mAh g-1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 5C, respectively, which are significantly higher than those of the Ni@CNT-S and Co@CNT-S based cell, especially at high rates. When the current density returned to 0.1C, the discharge-specific capacity of the NiCo@CNT-S based cell recovered to 1322 mAhg-1, demonstrating exceptional electrochemical reversibility. Besides, d shows a distinct liquid-solid conversion platform in the GCD profiles of NiCo@CNT-S based cell at different rates, indicating its excellent reaction kinetics attributed to the advanced electrode matrix design.Subsequently, the long-term cycling performance of NiCo@CNT-S based cell was evaluated at 3C and 5C (e), with initial specific capacities of 1047 and 1030 mAh g-1, respectively, demonstrating its strong capacity even at high rates. The discharge-specific capacity of NiCo@CNT-S based cell still maintains 927 mAhg-1 and 787 mAhg-1 after 100 cycles at 3C and 5C, confirming its exceptional electrochemical performance and long-term cycling stability. Furthermore, we compared therate performancesof NiCo@CNT-S based cellwith previously reported studies as shown in f and Table S1. In short, high rate performanceLi-S batteryhas been successfully constructed.

[Paragraph 22]
Currently, the pursuit of high energy density Li-S batteries is strongly associated with high sulfur loading and low electrolyte dosage. As shown in g, the cell equipped with NiCo@CNT-S cathode exhibits an outstanding areal rate performance with a superb discharge capacity of 4.4 mAh cm-2 at 0.1C with a sulfur loading of 5.1 mgcm-2 and an E/S ratio of 13.9 μL mg-1. Notably, by further increasing the sulfur loading to 7.6 mg cm-2 and decreasing the E/S to 9.3 μL mg-1, the cell equipped with NiCo@CNT-S cathode also achieved an average discharge capacity of 5.0 mAh cm-2 at 0.1C in 100 cycles (h).

[Paragraph 23]
To assess the distinct catalytic activities of Ni@CNT-S, Co@CNT-S, and NiCo@CNT-S based cells on theredox reactionsof LiPSs, CV profiles were investigated at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s-1 ( a). The two sharp cathodic peaks around 2.3 and 2.0 V correspond to the reduction of S8 to soluble long-chain LiPSs (Li2Sn, 4 ≤ n ≤ 8) and the further reduction to insoluble Li2S/Li2S2, respectively, which has been further verified by the in-situ Raman spectroscopy of NiCo@CNT-S during cathodic scan (Fig. S6). The two overlapping anodic peaks in the range of 2.3 to 2.4 V correspond to the oxidation of Li2S/Li2S2 to soluble long-chain LiPSs followed by conversion to S8. The reduction and oxidation peaks of NiCo@CNT-S based cell show a positive and negative shift, with a smaller voltage difference between the redox peaks, indicating enhanced redox kinetics for LiPSs. The Tafel curves further evaluate the electrocatalytic properties of thesulfur host during LiPSs redox(b-e). The cell equipped with NiCo@CNT-S cathode exhibits the lowest fitting slopes (126.4, 60.3, and 41.4 mV dec-1) among all peaks, indicating the rapid redox reaction kinetics for LiPSs. More importantly, the fitting slope of Co@CNT-S based cell at peak O is significantly smaller than that of Ni@CNT-S based cell, indicating that Co@CNT based cell is more favorable for SOR to accelerate Li2S dissolution. Meanwhile, the fitting slopes of Ni@CNT-S based cell at peak R are markedly smaller than those of Co@CNT-S based cell, suggesting that Ni@CNT based cell is more beneficial for SRR to accelerate Li2S deposition. NiCo@CNT based cell combines the advantages of both Ni@CNT and Co@CNT based cells, enabling bidirectional redox catalytic effect.

[Paragraph 24]
f-i shows the polarization voltage (ΔEa), Li2S nucleation overpotential (ΔEb), and Li2S activation overpotential (ΔEc) for all cathodes based on the GCD profiles at 0.1C. The abrupt potential drop at the onset of the second discharge plateau indicates the nucleation overpotential of insoluble Li2S2/Li2S, while the brief voltage spike at the beginning of the initial charging process reflects the activation overpotential of Li2S. As shown in g, the nucleation overpotential of Li2S2/Li2S for Ni@CNT-S based cell is significantly lower than that of Co@CNT-S based cell, indicating that Ni@CNT-S based cell facilitates the Li2S nucleation process in the SRR. Meanwhile, the activation overpotential of Li2S2/Li2S for Co@CNT-S based cell is significantly lower than that of Ni@CNT-S based cell, indicating that Co@CNT-S based cell effectively supports the Li2S dissolution process in the SOR (h), which is consistent with the Tafel curve results above. Overall, due to the cooperative interaction of Ni@CNT-S and Co@CNT-S, NiCo@CNT-S based cell exhibits the lowest values of ΔEa, ΔEb, and ΔEc, which indicates that: i) NiCo@CNT-S based cell demonstrates the highest electrocatalytic activity, facilitating the conversion reaction kinetics of LiPSs; and ii) NiCo@CNT-S based cell promotes the nucleation and activation of Li2S on its surface by more effectively lowering the nucleation/activation overpotential of Li2S, thereby preventing uncontrollable Li2S deposition.

[Paragraph 25]
As shown in j-l and n-p, Li2S nucleation and dissolution experiments reveal that NiCo@CNT-S based cell achieves the maximum deposition (300.7 mAhg-1) and dissolution capacities (1329.1 mAhg-1), verifying its superior catalytic activity for LiPSs oxidation and reduction. Interestingly, the Li2S deposition capacity of Ni@CNT-S based cell (212.9 mAhg-1) is significantly higher than that of Co@CNT-S based cell (188.5 mAhg-1), indicating that Ni@CNT-S based cell is more beneficial for the SRR. Meanwhile, the Li2S dissolution capacity of Co@CNT-S based cell (970.8 mAhg-1) is notably greater than that of Ni@CNT-S based cell (897.0 mAhg-1), suggesting that Co@CNT-S based cell is more conducive for the SOR. These results are consistent with the discussions on the Tafel curve and overpotential above. The CV curves of the symmetric cells (m and Fig. S7) indicate that NiCo@CNT-S based cell exhibit significantly higher redox current than Ni@CNT-S and Co@CNT-S based cellswithin the voltage range of -0.8 V to 0.8 V at a scan rate of 0.5 mV s-1 and 50 mV s-1, confirming that the synergistic effect of NiCo bimetallic catalysts facilitates the conversion of LiPSs and accelerates the kinetics of electrochemical reactions in Li-S batteries. These experimental findings are consistent with the theoretical calculations on the synergistic enhancement of the catalytic activity in the NiCo bimetallic system.

[Paragraph 26]
Electrical conductivity is a critical factor influencing the rate performance of batteries. The electronic conductivity of Ni@CNT-S, Co@CNT-S, and NiCo@CNT-S based cells was quantified through conductivity measurements, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements before and after cycling, and electron transfer number.  a shows the electronic conductivity of the cathode materials measured using a sandwich structure (Fig. S8). The electronic conductivity of NiCo@CNT is 1.9 S m-1, which is significantly higher than Ni@CNT (1.6 S m-1) and Co@CNT (0.9 S m-1). The enhanced electronic conductivity of NiCo@CNT could facilitate fast electron transport and contribute to the high rate performance. Subsequently, EIS was performed on the coin cells assembled with Ni@CNT-S, Co@CNT-S, and NiCo@CNT-S. b displays the Nyquist plot of the battery under open circuit voltage (OCV), showing a semicircle in the high-frequency region and a diagonal line in the low-frequency region. The semicircle diameter in the high-frequency region represents the charge transfer resistance (Rct), while the diagonal line in the low-frequency region indicates the semi-infinite Warburg diffusion of Li+ within the electrode. As shown in Fig. S9a and Table S2, in comparison to Ni@CNT-S (48.2 Ω) and Co@CNT-S (69.7 Ω), NiCo@CNT-S based cell exhibits the lowest charge transfer resistance (Rct) of 35.4 Ω, indicating that the excellent conductivity of NiCo@CNT facilitates Li+ diffusion and the transport of electrons. Additionally, it is noteworthy that the EIS measurements of all cathodes (c) were conducted after 100 cycles at 1C. The intercept on the Z' axis represents the combined resistance (Rb), which includes the intrinsic resistance of the active material, the ionic resistance of the electrolyte, and the contact resistance between the active material and the substrate. The two semicircles observed at higher frequencies are attributed to the interfacial impedance, which includes the resistance of the solid electrolyte interphase (RSEI) and the charge transfer resistance (Rct). The impedance fitting results (Fig. S9b and Table S2) show that NiCo@CNT-S based cell has the lowest RSEI and Rct, which suggests that it could effectively accelerate the conversion of LiPSs and reduce the dead sulfur generation. This is primarily attributed to its excellent conductivity during the cycling process, facilitating capacity release under high current conditions.

[Paragraph 27]
To investigate the role of conductivity during the cycling, the electron transfer number of the reduction process was investigated. Integration of the CV peaks (a and d-f) clearly shows that NiCo@CNT-S based cell has a higher electron transfer number at peaks R1 and R2 (3.6/10.0) (Table S3-S5), primarily attributing to the fact that NiCo@CNT-S based cell possesses excellent electrical conductivity. Detailed analysis reveals that Ni@CNT-S based cell contributes more electron transfer number in the high current region I, whereas Co@CNT-S based cell shows a greater electron transfer number in the low current region II. NiCo@CNT-S based cell combines the advantages of both Ni@CNT-S and Co@CNT-S based cells, achieving the highest electron transfer number, indicating superior conductive properties that support high-rate charge and discharge.

To explore the chemical adsorption capacities of Ni@CNT, Co@CNT, and NiCo@CNT for LiPSs, the polysulfide visualization adsorption experiment was conducted. To demonstrate the application of NiCo@CNT-S based cell in flexible electronic devices, a lithium foil was assembled as the anode into a highly flexible pouch cell packaged with aluminum-plastic film. As shown in b, the pouch cell achieves a specific discharge capacity of 8.2 mAh cm-2 at 0.1C with a sulfur loading of 7.1 mg cm-2 and a low E/S ratio of 7.1 μL mg-1. The LED lights continue to work in various bending states of the cells, including flat, 90°, 180°, and curled positions, indicating the promising potential of flexible NiCo@CNT-S based pouch cell for applications in wearable electronics and portable medical devices.

Conclusion

In summary, we designed and synthesized a unique bimetal-porous carbon network skeleton composite structure based on CC as a flexible self-supporting sulfur host, which consists of interconnected CNTs and NiCo bimetallic particles encapsulated within the CNTs. The 3D porous carbon conductive network skeleton formed by crosslinked CNTs effectively alleviates the sulfur volume expansion problem, and accommodates abundant active materials for high discharge capacity. Furthermore, the NiCo bimetals synergistically combine the catalytic effects of Ni in sulfur reduction and Co in sulfur oxidation, delivering an integrated bidirectional redox catalytic effect. Most importantly, the sulfur host facilitates a synergistic interaction between the conductive CNTs and the catalytically active bimetallic component, thereby enhancing electrical conductivity and accelerating electrochemical reaction kinetics, ultimately yielding a substantial improvement in rate capability. Consequently,the cell equipped with NiCo@CNT-S cathode achieves an initial specific capacity as high as 1030 mAh g-1 at 5C, maintaining a remarkable discharge capacity of 787 mAh g-1 after 100 cycles. Notably, the pouch cell assembled with the NiCo@CNT-S delivered a high areal discharge capacity of 8.2 mAh cm-2 at 0.1C, with a sulfur loading of 7.1 mg cm-2 and an E/S ratio of 7.1 μL mg-1. Overall, this unique 3D porous conductive networkframework decorated with CNTs encapsulating bimetalsholds great promise for designing next-generation high-performance Li-S batteries with high energy density and excellent rate performance.
