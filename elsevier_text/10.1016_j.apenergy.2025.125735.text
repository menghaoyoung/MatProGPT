[Paragraph 14]
Differential voltage analysis (DVA) is a non-destructive method for analyzing the degradation mechanisms of lithium-ion batteries and is widely used. The capacity variations between the peaks on the differential voltage curves are considered to reflect different degradation mechanisms of the batteries. However, DVA of batteries is the coupling of cathodes and anodes, and is based on idealized assumptions that still require experimental validation and correction. Due to the challenges in fabricating long-term and reliable reference electrodes for commercial batteries, it is hard to decouple the cathode and anode over the battery entire lifespan. In this study, four types of three-electrode batteries are fabricated. The cycling aging experiments are conducted on the three-electrode batteries, decoupling the voltages of the cathode and anode over the entire battery lifespan. The DVA method is validated and corrected. The corrected DVA, combined with electrochemical impedance spectroscopy based on reference electrodes, is used to analyze and quantify the battery degradation mechanisms. The degradation mechanisms of 36.3 % loss of lithium inventory, 7.8 % loss of active material (LAM) for anode, and severe LAM for cathode are identified. Finally, the degradation mechanisms are validated by post-mortem analysis. This study provides valuable insights and guidance for the use of DVA and the future commercial three-electrode batteries in battery diagnosis and prognosis.

Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), with outstanding energy density, power density, and economic advantages, are driving the rapid development and application of energy storage systems and electric vehicles, profoundly transforming society today []. However, LIBs experience various forms of degradation during cycling and storage aging, leading to a decrease in available capacity. These include the loss of active material (LAM) in the anode and cathode, loss of lithium inventory (LLI), electrolyte consumption, and increased impedance, among others []. For large-capacity battery systems such as grid energy storage and electric vehicles, accurate degradation mechanism analysis is essential. It enables comprehensive evaluation and prediction of the battery lifespan, enhancing the reliability of the equipment []. Moreover, it allows for duly adjusting the battery management strategy based on the degradation mechanisms, thereby extending the battery service life [].

[Paragraph 16]
Differential voltage analysis (DVA) is a non-destructive method for analyzing the degradation mechanisms of LIBs [,]. By applying differentiation to the pseudo open circuit voltage (OCV) vs. capacity, the dV/dQ vs. capacity is obtained []. The peaks on the differential voltage (DV) curves represent the material being in the single-phase, and the distance variations between the peaks on the DV curves are considered to reflect different degradation mechanisms of batteries []. For example, LLI can lead to changes in the operating voltage window of the anode and cathode, resulting in a relative shift of the anode and cathode peaks on DV curves. LAM in the anode and cathode can cause variations in the distances between peaks in the anode and cathode DV curves. DVA was first proposed by Boolm et al. [,] and has since been widely applied in the degradation mechanism analysis of various types of LIBs, including Li(NiCoMn)O₂/graphite (NCM/Gr) [,], Li(NiCoAl)O₂/graphite (NCA/Gr) [], NCM blended with LiMn₂O₄ (LMO)/graphite, LiCoO₂/graphite (LCO/Gr) [], and LiFePO₄/graphite (LFP/Gr) [] batteries. However, DVA is generally performed on commercial two-electrode batteries. The cell voltage is the voltage difference between the anode and cathode, meaning that the cell DVA curve is the result of the coupling between the anode and cathode. DVA is based on idealized assumptions, such as the independence and proportional changes of the anode and cathode DV curves. As batteries undergo cycling aging, it remains unclear whether the positions of the peaks on the anode and cathode DV curves will differ on the full cell DV curves, and which capacity variations between the peaks on the charge and discharge DV curves can more effectively quantify the degradation mechanisms of batteries. These still require experiments to validate and correct. Therefore, decoupling the voltages of the cathode and anode during the battery cycling aging is necessary.

[Paragraph 17]
Reference electrodes can enable decoupling the voltages of the cathode and anode []. Various types of reference electrode designs have been reported, including different material types such as lithium metal, lithium alloys (Li-Al, Li-Au) [,], and intercalation oxides (Li4Ti5O12, LFP) [], as well as various structural designs such as filamentous, mesh, and sheet forms [], along with different placement positions [,]. These studies were focused on developing reference electrodes that provide accurate potential and function for as long as possible. []. Due to the high complexity of fabricating three-electrode batteries and the challenges in ensuring the lifespan and reliability of the reference electrodes, few studies have utilized reference electrodes to measure and decouple the voltages of the anode and cathode over the entire battery lifespan. Liu et al. [] employed an in-situ reference electrode measurement setup, in which the cap of the 18,650 battery was removed, and an external lithium reference electrode, along with the battery, was immersed in the electrolyte. The anode voltage curves of the battery at different cycles were obtained. Diao et al. [] conducted cycling experiments using customized three-electrode commercial batteries, where an L-shaped copper foil reference electrode with plated lithium was placed between the electrode stack and the pouch case. However, in these studies, the reference electrodes were positioned outside the battery or electrode stack, leading to insufficient accuracy. Additionally, the analysis and validation of the DVA method were not studied.

[Paragraph 18]
Therefore, in this study, four types of three-electrode batteries are prepared, combining two types of reference electrodes and two types of pouch batteries. The cycling aging experiments are conducted on these three-electrode batteries, achieving decoupling the voltages of the cathode and anode over the entire battery lifespan. The false peaks of the anode and cathode DV curves in three-electrode batteries are revealed. Using the small current charge and discharge voltage curves of the anode, cathode, and full cell obtained during battery cycling aging, the DVA method in analyzing the degradation mechanisms of LIBs is validated and corrected. Subsequently, based on the corrected DVA method and three-electrode EIS analysis, the degradation mechanisms of the batteries are analyzed. Finally, the degradation mechanisms are validated through battery disassembly, coin half-cell assembly and tests, as well as material characterization techniques including SEM, EDS, and XPS. Experiments

Preparation of three-electrode batteries

A total of four types of three-electrode batteries are prepared, as shown in  , using combinations of two types of batteries and two types of reference electrodes. One battery type is a single-layer pouch cell, with Li(Ni0.5Co0.2Mn0.3)O₂ (NCM523) as the cathode material and Gr as the anode. The electrodes are single-side coated from Hefei Kejing Material Technology Co., Ltd. The other battery type is a jelly-roll pouch cell with the same electrode materials without electrolyte from Lifang Technology Co., Ltd. The two types of reference electrodes are copper wire coated with Li4T5O12 (LTO) and copper wire in situ lithium-plated. The LTO reference electrodes are sourced from the 21C LAB of Contemporary Amperex Technology Co., Ltd. They are based on 0.2 mm diameter insulated copper wires with both ends stripped of insulation and one end coated with LTO active material. The in situ lithium-plated reference electrodes are prepared in our laboratory. They are based on 0.02 mm diameter insulated copper wires. Both ends of the copper wires, 1 cm in length, are soaked in the concentrated sulfuric acid solution for 15 min, then cleaned with deionized water to remove the insulation layers. The treated copper wires are not coated with active material. Instead, lithium ions from the batteries are in situ plated onto the copper wires within the cells, forming the lithium metal reference electrodes. For the single-layer pouch cells, the two types of reference electrodes are placed between the anode and cathode, respectively, separated by separators. The electrode stacks are then sealed with aluminum-plastic films. Nickel pieces are welded onto the exposed copper wires outside the batteries for easy connection, forming the three-electrode batteries A and B, as shown in  (a) and (b), respectively. For the jelly-roll pouch cell, before electrolyte injection, the aluminum-plastic films of batteries are peeled open, and the jelly-roll electrodes are unfolded into two layers. The two types of reference electrodes are then placed between the anode and cathode, with separators isolating them from the electrodes. The jelly-roll electrodes are then restored to their original shape, resealed with aluminum-plastic films, and finally, the reference electrodes are welded with nickel pieces, forming the three-electrode batteries C and D, as shown in  (c) and (d), respectively. The four types of three-electrode batteries use the same electrolyte (1 M LiPF₆ in dimethyl carbonate (DMC): ethylene carbonate (EC): ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) = 1:1:1 Vol% with 1 % vinylene carbonate (VC)), with 0.7 g of electrolyte injected into batteries A and B, and 4 g of electrolyte injected into batteries C and D. The detailed process for the preparation of laboratory pouch cells can be found in our previous research []. To ensure good contact between the electrodes, all batteries are clamped with two acrylic plates equipped with foam pads. The detailed specifications of the four types of batteries are listed in  .

[Paragraph 20]
After electrolyte injection, the batteries undergo formation, degassing, and resealing steps. The reference electrodes are then activated or generated using the battery testing device (Maccor). For batteries A and C, at 50 % state of charge (SoC), the reference electrode and the anode are connected to the negative and positive terminals of the battery testing device, respectively, and a 2 μA constant current (CC) charge is applied for 4 h to activate the LTO reference electrode. For batteries B and D, the same method is used to form the in situ lithium-plated reference electrodes.

[Paragraph 21]
Aging and electrochemical tests

After the activation of the reference electrodes, the four types of three-electrode batteries undergo CC charge and discharge between 2.5 V and 4.2 V at a 0.05C rate. The voltage between the anode and reference electrode, as well as between the cathode and reference electrode, is measured through auxiliary channels. These voltages are used to form the DV curves for the anodes, cathodes, and full cells at the beginning of life (BoL) for the batteries. Since the LTO reference electrode is less active than the lithium metal reference electrode and has fewer parasitic reactions with the electrolyte, it has the longer lifespan. Therefore, Group A, which uses the LTO reference electrodes, was selected for the accelerated cycling aging experiments. Group A consists of four cells, named A_1 to A_4. The accelerated aging tests are conducted on cells A_1 to A_3, with a 1C rate, a voltage window of 3 V to 4.2 V. The tests involve constant current-constant voltage (CC-CV) charging and constant current (CC) discharging, with a 30-min rest period between cycles. During the cycling aging process, the small testing current can cause lithium ions in the LTO reference electrode to dissolve into the electrolyte, leading to a drift in the reference electrode potential, as shown in Fig. S1(b). Therefore, every 50 cycles, the reference electrodes are reactivated by applying a 2 μA CC charge for 2 h. The method of reactivating the reference electrodes and the voltage changes during the reactivation process are shown in Figs. S1(a) and (c), respectively. After reactivation, a 0.2C rate CC charge and CC discharge are performed to test the capacities of the cells. Additionally, a 0.05C rate CC charge and discharge are conducted for one cycle to generate the DV curves of the anode, cathode, and full cell during the aging process. The cells are then charged to 100 % SoC and discharged to 0 % SoC at the 0.2C rate for EIS tests, measuring the EISs of the anodes, cathodes, and full cells during the aging process. The EIS tests are conducted (Biologic VMP3) with a sinusoidal voltage signal of 5 mV and a frequency range from 400 kHz to 2 mHz. Before EIS tests, the cells are allowed to rest for at least 2 h to ensure they reach a steady state. Cells A_1 to A_3 are cycled up to 500 cycles. All tests are conducted in the 25 °C climate chamber (Binder).

[Paragraph 22]
Battery disassembly and coin half-cell tests

The discharged fresh cell A_4 and the aged cell A_1 (after 500 cycles) are disassembled inside the glove box (MBraun). The anodes, cathodes, and separators are separated and immersed in the DMC solution for 2 h to remove the residual electrolyte. Then the electrodes are dried inside the glove box. The anodes and cathodes of the cells are each cut into 6 mm diameter electrode plates. They are combined with a lithium metal plate (15.6 mm in diameter and 0.45 mm in thickness), a separator (19 mm in diameter and 25 μm in thickness), a nickel foam plate (16 mm in diameter and 0.5 mm in thickness), and a CR2016 casing to form three coin half-cells for each electrode. The half-cells use the same electrolyte as the pouch cells. The assembled half-cells are first left to rest for 10 h. Then, the anode and cathode half-cells are CC charged and discharged for three cycles at 0.1 mA between 3 V-4.3 V for the cathodes and 0.01 V-2 V for the anodes to measure the capacities of the half-cells. After this, the half-cells are adjusted to 100 % and 0 % SoC using 0.1 mA current, and EIS tests are conducted with the same test parameters as for the full cells. Finally, the half-cells undergo CC-CV charge and CC discharge tests for three cycles each at rates of 0.2C, 0.1C, 0.5C, 1C, 2C, and 0.2C to evaluate the rate performance of the anodes and cathodes.

[Paragraph 23]
SEM-EDS and XPS tests

Inside the glove box, small pieces of the disassembled electrodes are transferred to a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Regulus 8100) via the vacuum transfer chamber for morphological analysis. At the same time, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Bruker 60 mm2) is used to analyze the elemental composition and distribution on the surfaces of the electrodes. The electrodes are transferred with the same method to an X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI 5000 VersaProbe III) system to obtain compositional information. XPS tests are performed on the surface and at a 5 nm depth for the cathode samples and on the surface and at a 50 nm depth for the anode samples.

Results and discussion

DV curves in three-electrode batteries

The DV curve is represented as dV/dQ = f(Q). During the charge and discharge process of a battery, when phase transitions occur in the electrodes and two phases coexist, the voltages of electrodes manifest as plateaus. These are reflected as valleys on the DV curves. On the other hand, when the electrodes are in the single-phase regions, the voltages of electrodes change rapidly, which are reflected as peaks on the DV curves. The DV curve of a battery is the difference between the anode and cathode DV curves, that is:

[Paragraph 25]
(dV/dQ)cell = d(V cathode-V anode)/dQ = (dV/dQ)cathode-(dV/dQ)anode.

For the intuitive comparison between the charge and discharge DV curves, it can be further expressed as:

|(dV/dQ)cell| = |(dV/dQ)cathode| + |(dV/dQ)anode|.

The distance between the two peaks represents the capacity change between the two phase transitions. The contraction of this distance indicates electrode material loss, i.e., the presence of LAM. When LLI is present, it causes the relative shift in the voltage windows of the anode and cathode, leading to the relative displacement of the anode and cathode DV peaks. Based on the assumption that the electrode DV curves scale proportionally with the electrode capacities as the electrodes age and are independent of each other, the cell DV curves are commonly used for the non-destructive detection of LAM and LLI in LIBs. The steps for DVA are as follows: First, the battery is charged and discharged at a very low rate (C/20 or lower) to obtain the pseudo-OCV vs. capacity curves during aging. Then, the curves are differentiated to obtain the DV curves, and the movements of the peaks are analyzed.

[Paragraph 27]
The voltage curves of the anodes, cathodes, and full cells, along with the corresponding DV curves for the four types of batteries A to D at the BoL with a 0.05C rate CC discharge, are shown in  (a-d). In (a), it can be seen that the DV curve of the anode for battery A is consistent with the shape of the anode half-cell in (d). Clear P 1 and P 2 peaks are observed, as indicated by the green dots. However, the DV curve of the cathode of battery A does not match the shape of the cathode half-cell in (c). The DV curve of the cathode half-cell does not show obvious peaks, but in battery A, the peaks opposite to the anode peaks are exhibited in the cathode DV curve, as indicated by the red dots in (a). Similar phenomena are observed in the DV curves of batteries B to D. This is thought to arise from the blocking effect during the operation of three-electrode batteries. Studies have shown that the reference electrodes in three-electrode batteries can create the blocking effect, resulting in inhomogeneous intercalation currents near the reference electrodes [,]. This causes deviations in the electrolyte potential near the reference electrodes. The deviations in the local electrolyte potential are influenced by the local equilibrium potential of the electrode, where the equilibrium potential is more sensitive to lithium-ion concentration when its slope is large []. Consequently, the reference electrode is more prone to deviations when the anode is in the single-phase. The reference electrodes used in the experiments are very small in size, and the low current of 0.05C rate is applied to the batteries, minimizing the deviations of the reference electrodes. However, the DV amplifies even minor voltage fluctuations, leading to the false peaks of the cathodes. At the same time, deviations in the reference electrode potential may lead to slight changes in the peak height of the anode DV curves, but the positions of the peaks are unaffected. In contrast, due to the smooth variation in the equilibrium potential of the NCM523 cathode, no false peaks are observed in the anode DV curves. Therefore, it is recommended that when using reference electrodes for cathode and anode DVA, any false peaks introduced by the reference electrode blocking effect should first be excluded to avoid erroneous conclusions. Additionally, it is suggested to reduce the size of the reference electrodes, optimize their positioning, and decrease the current rate to mitigate the blocking effect of the reference electrodes, thereby minimizing the false peaks.

Degradation mechanism analysis

DVA method validation

The DV curves of cathodes, anodes, and full cells obtained during the cycling aging for cells A_1 to A_3 are used to validate and analyze the DVA method. The correlation between the positions of the peaks on full cell DV curves and the cathode and anode DV curves under the mutual influence of the cathode and anode during charging and discharging is validated. The correlation between the changes in the capacities observed between the peaks on the charge and discharge DV curves and the LLI and LAM is validated. Then, based on the corrected DVA method, the degradation mechanisms of the three-electrode batteries are analyzed.

The 0.05C charge and discharge voltage curves for the cathode, anode, and full cell of the representative cell A_1, obtained every 50 cycles, are shown in (b) and (c). The discharge voltage curves show that both the full cell and cathode discharge voltage curves shift downward and to the left with aging. The discharge voltage curves of the anode shift to the left horizontally with aging. The charge voltage curves show that the charge voltage curves of the full cell and cathode shift to the upper left. The charge voltage curves of the anode shape almost overlap, with only a shortening at the right end. The charge and discharge voltage curves indicate that the degree of lithiation in the anode at full charge is gradually decreasing. The discharge and charge DV curves of the full cell, cathode, and anode are shown in (d-i), respectively. The discharge DV curves of the full cell shift leftward and contract with aging, with the curve shape changing significantly, as shown within the dashed box in (d). The charge DV curves of the full cell contract leftward, with a significant change in shape at the end of the charge, as shown within the dashed box in (g). The discharge DV curves of the anode shift leftward, with the height of the peak P 1 gradually increasing, while the height of P 2 changes minimally, as indicated by the arrows in (e). The charge DV curves of the anode shorten at the right end, with minimal changes in both the position and height of peaks P 1 and P 2, as shown in (h). By combining the charge and discharge DV curves of the cathode, anode, and full cell, it can be observed that the shape changes of the full cell charge and discharge DV curves with aging are mainly influenced by the cathode, while the characteristic peaks are affected by the anode.

Since the NCM523 cathode DV curve does not exhibit characteristic peaks but only false peaks caused by the blocking effect, the detailed analysis is conducted only on the position changes of the characteristic peaks on the anode and full cell discharge and charge DV curves. The two characteristic peaks on the anode and full cell discharge DV curves are labeled as P 1 and P 2, with the discharge start and end points labeled as Start and End, respectively. The capacity corresponding to the Start to P 1 is labeled as L 1, from P 1 to P 2 as L 2, from P 2 to End as L 3, and from P 1 to End as L 4, as shown in (a). The P 1, P 2, and L 1 to L 4 on the charge DV curve correspond to those on the discharge DV curve, as shown in (b). The variation trends of L 1 to L 4 on the discharge DV curves are shown in (c), and the rates of change for L 2 to L 4 are shown in (e). The variation trends of L 1 to L 4 on the discharge DV curves of the anode and the cell are similar, but significant deviations are observed at 0, 250, and 300 cycles. This suggests that under the influence of the cathode, the peak positions on the full cell discharge DV curve do not fully correspond to the peak positions of the anode. The L 1 values for both the anode and full cell initially increase from 0 to 50 cycles, then continuously decrease after 50 cycles. L 2 and L 3 show a similar trend, with a significant decrease from 0 to 50 cycles, followed by stabilization. The rates of change for L 2 of the anode, L 2 of the full cell, L 4 of the anode, and L 4 of the full cell exhibit the same trend: they decrease by approximately -18 %, -37 %, -18 %, and - 25 %, respectively, from 0 to 50 cycles, and then stabilize. The L 3 for the anode decreases from 0 to 50 cycles, then stabilizes, with the rate of change first decreasing to -18 %, then stabilizing at around -18 %. For the full cell, the L 3 initially increases from 0 to 50 cycles, then stabilizes, with the rate of change first increasing to 20 %, then stabilizing at around 20 %. The variation trends of L 1 to L 4 on the charge DV curves are shown in (d), and the rates of change for L 2 to L 4 are shown in (f). The L 1 to L 4 for the anode and full cell charge DV curves correspond well, with only a small deviation at 0 cycles. This indicates that the peaks on the full cell charge DV curve accurately reflect the peaks on the anode. The L 1 for the anode and full cell continues to decrease from 0 to 500 cycles. For the anode, both L 2 and L 4 decrease from 0 to 50 cycles and then stabilize. Their rates of change decrease to around -5 % between the 0 to 50 cycles, and then fluctuate around -5 %. In contrast, L 2 and L 4 for the full cell steadily increase from 0 to 500 cycles, with their rates of change gradually increasing as well. The variation trends of L 3 for both the anode and full cell are similar to those of L 2 and L 4 for the anode, with the rates of change decreasing to -5 % from 0 to 50 cycles, and then fluctuating around -5 % thereafter.

[Paragraph 31]
In several studies, the change in L 1 of the cell discharge DV curve has been used to quantify LLI, and the change in L 2 has been used to quantify the anode LAM (LAMAN). However, this study suggests that the conclusion may contain some deviation for NCM523/graphite batteries. Firstly, the comparison of the charge and discharge DV curves reveals that the peak positions on the full cell charge DV curve more accurately reflect the peak positions for the anode. Secondly, the positions of the peak P 1 in the discharge DV curve vary significantly from 0 to 500 cycles, while the positions of the peak P 1 in the charge DV curve show relatively little variation. Kato et al. [] observed the same phenomenon in commercial batteries with Mn-based cathodes and graphite anodes. This is because, with cycling aging, when the battery experiences significant LLI, the amount of LiC6 for the graphite anode decreases at full charge. As the anode transitions from the two-phase region of LiC6 and LiC12 to the LiC12 single phase, the voltage undergoes a rapid change due to the insufficient supply of LiC6. As a result, the LiC12 peak on the discharge DV curve becomes sharper, and its position shifts significantly. However, this does not occur on the charge DV curve. Therefore, it is recommended to use the peaks on the full cell charge DV curve to identify the positions of the anode peaks when using DVA. It is evident that with aging, the lithium inventory of the cell decreases, which is consistent with the variation trend of L 1 on the charge DV curves for the anode and the full cell. Additionally, the active material of the anode quickly loses a portion due to solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation in the early stage, and then slowly degrades due to the slow thickening of SEI. This is consistent with the variation trends of L 2 and L 4 for the anode, as well as L 3 for both the anode and the full cell. However, it differs from the variation trends of L 2 and L 4 for the full cell. This is due to the presence of the cathode, which causes the peak P 1 of the anode to position deviation on the full cell. Therefore, in commercial NCM523/graphite batteries, it is recommended to use L 1 from the cell charge DV curve to quantify the LLI and L 3 to quantify the LAMAN.

[Paragraph 32]
Based on the change in L 1 of the full cell at 0 cycles (L 1_cycle0_cell) and 500 cycles (L 1_cycle500_cell), the capacity loss of the cell A_1 caused by LLI is calculated as:

 L 1_cycle0_cell-L 1_cycle500_cell = 15.7 mAh.

LLI accounts for 36.3 % of the initial capacity for the cell, which includes contributions from the SEI growth, the lithium plating, and the loss of active materials containing lithium. Based on the relative change in L 3 of the full cell from 0 cycles (L 3_cycle0_cell) to 500 cycles (L 3_cycle500_cell), the LAMAN is calculated as:

[Paragraph 33]
(L 3_cycle0_cell-L 3_cycle500_cell)/L 3_cycle0_cell*100 % = 7.8 %.

Using the same method, the LLI of cells A_2 and A_3 are 35.1 % and 29.3 %, respectively, and the LAMAN are 7.9 % and 7.3 %, respectively.

EIS results

At the BoL, the EISs of the full cell, cathode, and anode at 0 % and 100 % SOC are shown in (a) and (b), respectively. The EISs of the full cell, cathode, and anode exhibit regular shapes without distortion. It is demonstrated that the impedance of the cell is the sum of the impedances of the cathode and anode, indicating the impedance of the cell is successfully separated to the individual electrodes by the RE. Due to the decrease in cathode charge transfer resistance with increasing state of delithiation, the impedance of the full cell at 0 % SoC is significantly different from that at 100 % SoC. The EISs of cell A_1 at 0 % SoC for the full cell, cathode, and anode during cycling aging are shown in (c), (e), and (g), respectively. The EISs at 100 % SoC for the full cell, cathode, and anode during cycling aging are shown in (d), (f), and (h), respectively. The EISs of the full cell and cathode exhibit two regular semicircles, which increase in size with aging, indicating an increase in the impedance of the cell and cathode. The EISs of the anode show two irregular semicircles, which gradually increase from 50 to 500 cycles. Moreover, the shapes of the anode EISs gradually become irregular, which is attributed to lithium plating on the anode. The impedance of the anode also shows an increasing trend with aging.

[Paragraph 34]
The EISs are fitted using the equivalent circuit model shown in Fig. S2. R 0 represents the Ohmic resistance. R 1 in parallel with CPE 1 represents the migration of lithium ions through the interface layer in the high-frequency range. CPE stands for the constant phase element. R 2 in parallel with CPE 2 simulates the charge transfer process in the medium-frequency range. W represents the Warburg impedance, which corresponds to semi-infinite diffusion in the low-frequency range. The R 0, R 1, and R 2 fitting results for the full cell, cathode, and anode at 0 % and 100 % SoC are shown in (a), (d), and (b), (e), and (c), (f), respectively. The embedded images are magnified views of the R 1 and R 2 for the anode. (a) and (d) show that with aging, the values of Ohmic resistance R 0 for both the cathode and anode increase linearly at both 0 % and 100 % SoC. Additionally, the R 0 values for the cathode and anode are nearly equal. (b) and (e) show that with aging, the values of the cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) impedance R 1 for the cathode increase rapidly. At 0 % and 100 % SoC, the R 1 values of the cathode rise from 0.73 Ω and 0.42 Ω to 7.79 Ω and 6.58 Ω, respectively. In contrast, the values of SEI impedance R 1 for the anode increase much less. At 0 % and 100 % SoC, the R 1 values of the anode gradually increase from 0.016 Ω and 0.015 Ω to 0.076 Ω and 0.097 Ω, respectively. (c) and (f) show that with aging, the values of charge transfer impedance R 2 for the cathode increase significantly. At 0 % and 100 % SoC, the R 2 values of the cathode rise from 1.78 Ω and 0.29 Ω to 11.25 Ω and 14.93 Ω, respectively. In contrast, the R 2 values of the anode increase slowly with aging, at 0 % and 100 % SoC, rising from 0.30 Ω and 0.21 Ω to 0.60 Ω and 0.51 Ω, respectively. The EIS fitting results show that, with cycling aging, the impedances of R 0, R 1, and R 2 for both the cathode and anode increase. However, the interfacial impedance and charge transfer impedances are primarily attributed to the cathode, which exhibits significant degradation, while the anode shows less degradation. This is consistent with the DVA results, which indicate that the capacity degradation of the anode is relatively small.

Post-mortem analysis

The disassembly of the fresh cell A_4 (BoL) and aged cell A_1 (Aged) is conducted to analyze the degradation mechanisms for the anode and cathode. The disassembled electrode images of the cathode and anode for BoL are shown in Figs. S3 (a-b), and those of the Aged are shown in Figs. S3 (c-d). It can be observed that the surface of the BoL cathode is very smooth with no abnormalities, while the anode shows a small amount of lithium plating. This may be due to poor local contact between the anode and cathode during formation, likely caused by gas generation. The surface of the Aged cathode is also very smooth and flat, while the anode shows extensive lithium plating. This is because the cell electrodes of both the cathode and anode are single-layer, and during cycling, the cell temperature rise is minimal. Compared to jelly-roll and multi-layer cells, this makes it easier for lithium plating to occur at a 1C rate of cycling.

[Paragraph 36]
The disassembled electrodes are reassembled into coin half-cells inside the glove box. The capacity tests of the half-cells are shown in (d). The average capacity of the BoL cathode half-cells is 2.18 mAh, while the average capacity of the Aged cathode half-cells is around 1.48 mAh. The average capacity of BoL anode half-cells is 2.04 mAh, and the average capacity of Aged anode half-cells is 1.76 mAh. The rate performance tests are conducted on the BoL and Aged half-cells. In the first three cycles at a 0.2C rate, the discharge capacities of the BoL cathode and anode half-cells are approximately 2.1 mAh and 1.9 mAh, respectively. The discharge capacities of the Aged cathode half-cell and anode half-cell are approximately 0.60 mAh and 0.68 mAh, respectively. In the subsequent rate tests at 0.1C, 0.5C, 1C, and 2C for three cycles each, the discharge capacities of the BoL cathode half-cell decrease progressively. The discharge capacities of the Aged cathode half-cell become almost zero at 0.5C rate, indicating severe polarization in the cathode. The discharge capacities of the BoL anode half-cell decrease progressively, while the discharge capacities of the Aged anode half-cell are almost zero at the 1C rate, indicating that the anode polarization has also increased. However, the polarization of the anode is smaller than that of the cathode. The discharge capacities of all half-cells during the last three cycles at the 0.2C rate are nearly the same as those during the first three cycles at the 0.2C rate, proving the half-cells are not damaged during the rate performance tests. The EIS results of the BoL and Aged cathode and anode half-cells at 0 % SoC and 100 % SoC are shown in (b) and (e). The shapes of EISs for the Aged cathode and anode half-cells have significantly expanded compared to those of the BoL, with the cathode showing a more severe increase than the anode. This is consistent with the EIS results of the three-electrode cell. The DV curves of the cathode half-cells are shown in (c). Compared to the BoL, the Aged curve shows significant changes in shape, with an overall shift toward the upper left. The anode DV curves are shown in (f), where the peaks P 1 and P 2 of the Aged DV curve can be clearly observed. Additionally, the aged DV curve shifts left and contracts compared to the BoL. The comparison of the DV curves of the cathode and anode half-cells for BoL and Aged reveals that the shift of the cathode is significantly greater than that of the anode. The half-cell test results indicate that both the cathode and anode of the cell show degradation, but the degradation of the cathode is more severe.

[Paragraph 37]
Further microscopic morphological analysis is conducted to observe the changes in the cathodes and anodes before and after degradation through SEM. The 10,000× magnification images of the BoL and Aged cathodes are shown in (a) and (g), respectively, with the locally magnified areas of white dashed boxes shown in (c) and (i). (b) and (h) are images of the electron backscatter diffraction mode for the same areas, where the active material particles can be observed more clearly. It can be observed that, compared to the BoL cathode, the particle sizes of the active material in the Aged cathode have expanded, as indicated by the double-arrow lines in (b) and (h). The gaps between the active material particles have narrowed, as shown by the triangular markers in (b) and (h). This indicates that, with aging, the cathode active material has expanded, leading to material loss [,]. Additionally, the smoothness of the surface for the active material particles has decreased, which could be related to the thickening of the CEI. The morphology images of the anodes at 2000× magnification for BoL and Aged are shown in (d-f) and (j-l), respectively. The BoL anode exhibits a clear graphite structure, while the surface of the Aged anode is covered with a material, indicating the formation of the SEI. The EDS results of the BoL and Aged anodes are shown in Figs. S4(a) and (b), respectively. The weight percentages of each element are summarized in Fig. S4(c). In the Aged anode, Ni and Mn elements are observed to be present at approximately 1.09 % and 1.25 %, respectively, indicating significant dissolution of transition metals from the cathode, which then deposit onto the surface of the anode. Additionally, an increased proportion of O element in the Aged anode suggests thickening of the SEI on the anode. The EDS results of the BoL and Aged cathodes and the weight percentages of each element are shown in Figs. S4(d), (e), and (f), respectively. There are no significant differences in the elemental distribution or composition between them.

[Paragraph 38]
XPS is further employed to analyze the chemical composition of the electrode interfaces and investigate the degradation of both the anode and cathode. The XPS spectra of different elements are deconvoluted using the XPSpeak software. In the C 1 s spectra, four peaks are observed, located at approximately 284.8 eV, 286.6 eV, 288.9 eV, and 290.2 eV. These correspond to C-C/C-H, C-O, C=O, and Li₂CO₃, respectively. Li₂CO₃ represents the inorganic component of the SEI and CEI, while C-C/C-H, C-O, and C=O represent the organic components of the SEI and CEI. In the O 1 s spectra, four characteristic peaks are observed, located at 529 eV, 531.4 eV, 531.9 eV, and 533.3 eV, corresponding to metal oxides (M-O), Li₂CO₃, C=O, and C-O, respectively. In the F 1 s spectra, two peaks are found at 685.1 eV and 687.1 eV, which correspond to LiF and P-F, respectively. LiF originates from the decomposition of LiPF₆, while P-F is derived from the fluorophosphate components in the SEI and CEI [,].

[Paragraph 39]
The C 1 s, O 1 s, and F 1 s spectrum fitting results for the BoL anode surface and 50 nm depth are shown in  (a-c) and (d-f), respectively. The fitting results for the Aged anode surface and 50 nm depth are shown in (g-i) and (j-l), respectively. Comparing the anode surface and the 50 nm depth, it can be observed that in the C 1 s spectra, the proportions of Li2CO3, C-O, and C=O at the 50 nm depth are significantly reduced, suggesting that the SEI on the anode is very thin, possibly less than 50 nm depth. In the O 1 s spectra, a small amount of metal oxide, likely Li2O, is present on the surface and 50 nm depth, which is consistent with the observed lithium plating on the BoL anode in the disassembly image. In the F 1 s spectra, the proportion of P-F increases on the surface, indicating that the outer-layer SEI has more fluorophosphate salts. Comparing the surface and 50 nm depth of the Aged anode, the Li2CO3 content in the C 1 s spectra increases at the 50 nm depth compared to the surface. This suggests that more organic components of the inner-layer SEI have been converted into the more stable inorganic Li2CO3. It indicates that, with cycling aging, the SEI thickens and its composition changes. In the O 1 s spectra, a higher proportion of metal oxides is observed, which is Li2O and transition metal oxides from the cathode. This is consistent with the transition metal elements observed in the EDS results. In the F 1 s spectra, the increased proportion of P-F suggests that there are more LiF products inside the SEI. Compared to the BoL and Aged anodes, the proportion of Li2CO3 in the C 1 s spectra of the Aged anode decreases, indicating that the new SEI continuously forms on the anode surface, containing a large number of organic components, which is consistent with the conclusions from the anode morphological analysis. In the O 1 s spectra, the Aged anode shows a higher proportion of metal oxides than the BoL, suggesting that transition metals continue to leach out with aging. In the F 1 s spectra, there are no significant changes in the proportions of LiF and P-F between the BoL and Aged anodes.

[Paragraph 40]
The fitting results of the C 1 s, O 1 s, and F 1 s spectra for the BoL cathode surface and at the 5 nm depth are shown in  (a-c) and (d-f), respectively. The fitted results for the Aged cathode surface and at the 5 nm depth are shown in (g-i) and (j-l), respectively. Comparing the BoL cathode surface with the 5 nm depth, there are no significant changes in component proportions in the C 1 s spectra. In the O 1 s spectra, a certain amount of metal oxides is detected on the surface, originating from the cathode active material. At the 5 nm depth, the proportion of metal oxides increases, indicating a decrease in CEI components and suggesting that the BoL cathode CEI is relatively thin. In the F 1 s spectra, a higher proportion of P-F is observed on the surface, indicating that the surface CEI contains more fluorophosphate components. Comparing the Aged cathode surface with the 5 nm depth, there are no significant changes in component proportions in the C 1 s spectra. In the O 1 s spectra, the proportion of metal oxides on the surface decreases, while organic components and inorganic Li2CO3 increase. At the 5 nm depth, a certain proportion of metal oxides is still observable. In the F 1 s spectra, the proportion of P-F increases, suggesting that the inner-layer CEI contains more LiF, whereas the outer layer has more fluorophosphate compounds. Comparing the BoL and Aged cathode surfaces, no significant changes in component proportions are observed in the C 1 s spectra. In the O 1 s spectra, the proportion of metal oxides from the cathode active material in the Aged cathode decreases substantially compared to the BoL cathode, while inorganic components and organic Li2CO3 increase, indicating the thickening of CEI with aging. This is consistent with the decreased particle smoothness from the SEM images of the Aged cathode. In the F 1 s spectra, the Aged cathode surface shows relatively higher LiF content, suggesting that more LiPF6 decomposes and deposits on the cathode surface with aging.

[Paragraph 41]
The XPS results indicate that the thickness of the SEI or CEI on both the anode and cathode gradually increases with aging accompanied by compositional changes. On the surface of the aged anode, some deposited transition metal oxides originating from the cathode are observed, which contributes to the more severe degradation of the cathode.

[Paragraph 42]
Conclusion

In this study, four types of three-electrode cells are prepared to decouple the voltages of the cathode and anode over the entire battery lifespan. The false peaks on the DV curves of the cathode in three-electrode batteries are revealed. The cycling aging experiments are conducted to validate and correct the DVA method of non-destructive degradation analysis for LIBs. By employing the corrected DVA method in conjunction with EIS analysis based on the reference electrodes, the degradation mechanisms of experimental batteries are analyzed. Furthermore, the validation of the degradation mechanisms is performed through battery disassembly, coin half-cell tests, and material characterization via SEM, EDS, and XPS techniques. The main conclusions are as follows: (1) The blocking effect caused by reference electrodes can introduce false peaks of the cathode and anode DV curves at the single-phase regions of the electrodes for the three-electrode batteries. It is suggested to reduce the size of the reference electrodes, optimize their positioning, and decrease the current rate to minimize the false peaks caused by this blocking effect. (2) In the charge DV curve, the peak positions of the cell align well with those of the anode, whereas in the discharge DV curve, there are certain deviations between them. In two-electrode commercial batteries, it is recommended to use the charge DV curve for DVA. The absolute change of L 1 can effectively indicate LLI, while the relative change of L 3 better represents LAMNE compared to L 2 and L 4. (3) Based on the corrected DVA method, it is shown that the experimental cells exhibit the small LAMNE, with the primary loss being LLI, which includes lithium plating and the loss of active material containing lithium in the cathodes. The EIS analysis indicates that, with aging, most of the interfacial resistance and charge transfer resistance originate from the cathode, where LAM is more severe. (4) The post-mortem analysis reveals that, under the cycling of 1C rate, minimal temperature rise in the single-layer cells leads to significant lithium plating. The cathode experiences particle expansion degradation, transition metal dissolution, and CEI thickening, while the anode shows SEI thickening with comparatively less degradation. The post-mortem analysis validates the degradation mechanisms indicated by the corrected DVA method and three-electrode EIS analysis. This study provides valuable insights and guidance for the use of the DVA method and future commercial three-electrode cells in battery diagnosis and prognosis.
